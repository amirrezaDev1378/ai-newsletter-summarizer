# How to tell if a startup has a moat

*A framework for assessing whether the company you’re considering joining is built to last*

By [Dan Hockenmaier](https://substack.com/@danhock) (Co-authored with Lenny)

Moats! Does anyone have one anymore? As AI makes it easy to build anything, this is one of the biggest questions on people’s minds right now—especially people trying to decide where to work. 

There is a pervasive belief that startups are a lottery ticket. It’s impossible to pick winners, so it’s just a roll of the dice. But not only can startups be a great learning experience and a lot of fun, they can also provide economic rewards comparable to (or greater than) the “safe” route into big tech.

However, for this to be true, you must rigorously evaluate the startup you’re considering joining. A company can check all your boxes and look like a rocket ship, but if it doesn’t have a moat, it’s going to stall out. Moats are more important than ever in the AI era. 

Assessing a startup’s moat isn’t an exact science, but there is a simple framework that can meaningfully increase your odds of picking a winner:

1. **Identify a compounding advantage**
2. **Gauge how strong that advantage could be**
3. **Measure if it’s working**

## Step 1: Identify a compounding advantage

![Compounding Advantages](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!m9iH!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F14d3f803-627c-4490-99ab-bc9eb64a7a3b_1456x1484.png)

In reality, only a small subset of what people call moats actually drive the vast majority of long-term value. These are **compounding advantages**: not just a temporary edge, but *a self-reinforcing flywheel that gets stronger and stronger over time, ultimately making a company very hard to compete with.*

There are only four compounding advantages that really matter:

![The Four Compounding Advantages](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oaMP!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9f631590-b6b9-42b7-b035-59ce0fad659f_1456x1595.png)

1. **Switching costs:** The more deeply embedded a product is in the way a customer works, the harder it is to switch. This increases retention and revenue per customer, which can be reinvested into the product.
2. **Network effects:** The more users a product has, the better it gets for everyone. This increases engagement and attracts more users.
3. **Scale economies:** The more volume a company has, the larger and more efficient its operations can be. This lowers unit costs, funding lower prices or a better product at the same price.
4. **Data effects:** The more usage a product has, the more data it generates. This data can be used to improve the product and increase usage.

Speed of execution, distribution, brand, a great founding team, and lots of funding are **fuel**, but they are not compounding advantages. They get the car moving, but the compounding advantages are what make the car roll downhill on its own.

### How to identify the compounding advantage

Focus on what the team is *actually doing*. What is on their roadmap? Which teams are large and influential?

#### 1. Switching costs
To build this, a product must become so deeply embedded into how a customer works that they cannot remove it without severe disruption. 
* **What to look for:** A rich roadmap focused on better serving existing use cases, expanding surfaces, and building heavy integrations. The team should be heavily tilted toward product and engineering, shipping rapidly.
* **Examples:** Salesforce, Datadog, ADP, Epic, Rippling.

#### 2. Network effects
The value of a network-effect business increases with each additional user. UI and feature sets matter less than the network itself.
* **What to look for:** A focus on growing the network, improving search and discovery algorithms, and reducing transaction risks.
* **Examples:** Craigslist, LinkedIn, Roblox, Amazon, Faire.

#### 3. Scale economies
These businesses lower unit costs or raise quality through massive volume and capital investments.
* **What to look for:** Capital-intensive operations, large engineering/finance teams, and aggressive expansion into new geographies and categories.
* **Examples:** SpaceX, Costco, Netflix, frontier AI labs, Zipline.

#### 4. Data effects
Data acts as the core input that makes the product higher-quality, more efficient, or functional across more use cases.
* **What to look for:** Efforts to collect and sanitize data, with machine learning and data science acting as highly influential teams.
* **Examples:** Stripe Radar, Cursor.

## Step 2: Gauge how strong the compounding advantage could be

Once you have identified which compounding advantage the startup might have (e.g., a network effect), the next step is to assess how powerful that advantage can become. Not all compounding advantages are created equal—for example, every marketplace has some form of network effect, but many still fail to build sustainable businesses.
