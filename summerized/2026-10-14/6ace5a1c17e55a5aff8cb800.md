## 1. Disaster Recovery

*Excerpt from Chapter 10: Disaster Recovery.*

We’ve already discussed how to handle a variety of failures that impact individual service instances. But what happens when a catastrophic failure causes widespread outage of large parts of your system? Fire, flood, electrical supply issues, and errors in the underlying cloud infrastructure are all outside of your control, and at one fell swoop, they can render your attempts to make your system more robust totally redundant.

Handling these situations falls under the auspices of disaster recovery (DR), a set of procedures that should kick in when something really bad happens. As with much of what we’ve looked at in this book, trade-offs abound. As you reduce your period of acceptable downtime or data loss, cost and complexity increase, so picking the right approach for your system can be a delicate balancing act. 

### Cloud Region, Data Center, or Site?

The exact terminology you use to discuss your infrastructure will likely vary based on implementation details. When running on the public cloud, you’ll probably be familiar with the concept of a cloud region. When running on a private cloud or in a more traditional on-premises setup, you’ll think in terms of data centers.

To keep things simple, I’ll use the term **site** as a general descriptor of where your computing infrastructure lives.

### How Good Does Your DR Need to Be?

A simple way to define technical requirements around DR is to make use of two concrete measurable targets: recovery point objective (RPO) and recovery time objective (RTO). 

![Figure 10-1. The key objectives to define as part of selecting an appropriate DR strategy](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-Hph!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3af4a60c-dabe-4da4-b6e9-1227c4e7abfd_1318x392.png)

#### Recovery Point Objective
The recovery point objective (RPO) defines the maximum amount of time that can have passed since the data was last backed up or synchronized. Put another way, RPO defines the upper duration of potential data loss. 

#### Recovery Time Objective
The recovery time objective (RTO) defines how long it’s acceptable for your system to be unavailable in the wake of an incident. Your RTO includes all the time needed to recover data from the last recovery point, make computing resources available for your workloads, and redirect traffic to your new site.

### You Can’t Have Everything

If asked what RPO and RTO you’d want in a vacuum, you’d want an RPO of zero and an RTO of zero. But as you reduce your RPO and RTO, cost and complexity increase dramatically—and in technical terms, it’s not possible to guarantee a zero RPO or RTO in any case.

Different types of workloads might require different RPOs and RTOs. For example, a bank needs a low RPO and RTO for payment information and withdrawals, whereas the customer sign-up system could tolerate higher targets. Your target RPO and RTO should also exceed anything specified in any service level agreements (SLAs) you have.

### DR Objectives and SLOs

At first glance, RPO and RTO would appear to make for good service level objectives (SLOs). However, the reality is that we don’t experience disasters regularly enough to gather useful SLIs (service level indicators). We can define related SLOs around things like the speed of data replication, but SLOs only tangentially measure RPO and RTO.

## 2. Disaster Recovery Patterns

*Another excerpt from Chapter 10: Disaster Recovery.*

With the discussion of RPO and RTO in mind, let’s take a look at some common patterns used to implement DR.

![Figure 10-2. Overview of DR patterns](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!P4Ph!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fad7aee47-aa20-4fc7-8e43-ae2bf2360782_1320x716.png)

### Pattern: Backup and Restore
*Recovery by restoring data from a backup and loading it into a replacement system.*

![Figure 10-3. The backup and restore DR pattern](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!sl8H!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4c818d67-38f2-4d49-a15a-b45a8896d032_1316x346.png)

* **How it works:** 
  1. Back up data regularly to a separate site.
  2. Provision infrastructure on the failover site (ideally automated).
  3. Restore data from backup to the failover site.
  4. Switch traffic from the primary to the failover site.
* **When to use it:** Applicable where your primary site is unavailable, you can tolerate a long RTO, and you want to keep costs low. Regular tests of the backup and restore process are necessary.

### Pattern: Pilot Light Failover
*Data is constantly synced to a failover site, with infrastructure provisioned after failover.*

![Figure 10-4. The pilot light failover DR pattern](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2Jjt!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F11f6ee69-6374-40a9-b2f5-cebc53dd19eb_1914x610.png)

* **How it works:** 
  1. Replicate data continually.
  2. Provision compute infrastructure on the failover site (databases are already present).
  3. Switch traffic.
* **When to use it:** Allows for a much shorter RPO because of more frequent data replication, while also reducing RTO by eliminating data restoration time. Cost savings make sense mainly in public cloud setups.

### Pattern: Warm Failover
*Data is synced to a failover site, with at least some infrastructure already provisioned to handle traffic.*

![Figure 10-5. The warm failover DR pattern](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0q1H!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6262c7c8-0610-4a9f-b3eb-618faee54dfe_1910x722.png)

Data is continually replicated, with a subset of infrastructure prioritized for more critical capabilities already running to allow a faster failover.

### Pattern: Multisite Active-Active
*The system is distributed across two or more sites, with writes being able to be sent to any site.*

![Figure 10-6. The multisite active-active DR pattern](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FylH!,w_1100,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0186c82d-c3d9-4067-8bc4-fd19425248ca_1908x988.png)

Sidesteps the need for a failover, delivering the lowest RTO and RPO at a much higher cost and complexity.

## 3. Infrastructure Availability When Planning for DR

The nature of GenAI workloads for training and inference has driven increased demand for infrastructure, leading to reduced capacity, GPU shortages, and spiked memory costs. 

If your DR approach assumes you can dynamically provision infrastructure during a failure, you may find yourself fighting over scraps. Three ways to handle this:
1. **Reserve capacity:** Pay a fee to guarantee availability (e.g., AWS EC2 instances), though this isn't available for all cloud services.
2. **Prioritize critical services:** Allocate limited infrastructure to workloads with higher criticality based on service-by-service RTOs and RPOs.
3. **Run fully redundant infrastructure:** Keep a fully redundant set running at all times—costs can be mitigated if you find alternative uses for these resources (such as load-test environments or worker pools for batch processing) when not needed for production DR.

## 4. Resiliency of AI-powered software

*Excerpt from Chapter 7: Resiliency and AI*

### Model Provider Resiliency
Foundation model providers like OpenAI and Anthropic do not have a strong track record for high availability compared to traditional cloud vendors. 
* **Graceful degradation:** Ensure your software operates even if the model is unavailable, by limiting functionality or handling rate limits.
* **Backup vendors:** Use alternative channels (e.g., running OpenAI models on Azure, or Anthropic models via Google’s Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform).

### Model Changes
LLMs are nondeterministic, and small model updates or changing versions can fundamentally alter how prompts are handled. 
* **Solution:** Maintain a robust set of tests using traditional functional tests combined with AI evaluation tools (evals) to catch surprises before your users do.

### Observability
Latency for AI operations varies wildly due to provider performance, rate limiting, or prompt length. 
* **Solution:** Implement dynamic timeouts based on token counts or monitor streaming output to track when responses start coming back rather than waiting for the full response.

## 5. How to handle uncertainty

### Multimodel, Multivendor
Build your software to support changing models so you aren't locked into a single provider's pricing or availability. Note that mixing models from multiple vendors in a single execution flow can compound availability risks, as all models must function correctly simultaneously.

### Cost Transparency
Pay-as-you-go pricing models can lead to surprising bills. Providing visibility into spending (even on a per-person or per-engineer basis) helps teams understand usage value, optimize prompts, and spot catastrophic overspending before bills climb precipitously.

### More Frequent Vendor Analysis
Given the volatility of the AI sector, increase the frequency of your vendor management reviews and threat assessments. Check if your vendors are wholly dependent on a single upstream provider, which introduces secondary risk.

## Takeaways

* **AI makes "resiliency basics" more important:** As AI agents generate code and scale up system complexity, having proper monitoring, alerting, idempotency, retries, and timeout handling becomes critical.
* **Disaster recovery planning is non-negotiable for high-scale operations:** Major outages (like Coinbase going down due to a single AWS zone failure) highlight the danger of lacking automated failovers and robust DR rehearsals.
