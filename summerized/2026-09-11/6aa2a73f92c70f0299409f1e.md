# An Ode to Counter-Positioning: How to Buy Time

## Core Message
Startups cannot skip business strategy or ignore the need for moats simply because they are young. While early-stage companies lack access to advanced moats like network effects or massive scale economies, they can leverage **counter-positioning**—developing a business model that creates conflicting incentives for incumbents, preventing them from responding effectively—to buy critical time.

---

## Valuable Takeaways

* **What is Counter-Positioning?** 
  Defined by Hamilton Helmer (via *7 Powers*), it is the practice of shaping your business model so that incumbents are trapped by conflicting incentives, making it suicidal or impossible for them to copy you without damaging their existing profit centers.
* **Counter-Positioning as a "Take-Off" Power:** 
  It is a temporary source of power designed to help startups launch and disrupt incumbents. Once the window is bought, companies must transition to long-term moats (e.g., Scale Economies, Switching Costs, Brand, Network Effects).
* **Classic Examples of Counter-Positioning:**
  * **Ramp:** Built its corporate card model on helping customers *spend less* rather than incentivizing more spending through points, locking out incumbents whose core revenues relied on high-volume interchange fees.
  * **Base Power Company:** Sells electricity via home battery installations rather than selling the physical batteries themselves, upending traditional energy pricing models.
  * **Google (Android):** Gave away mobile operating systems for free because its revenue lived upstream in search, while competitors like Nokia relied on the hardware stack itself.
  * **Microsoft (vs. IBM):** Licensed DOS non-exclusively and benefited from the commoditization of hardware, successfully destroying IBM's integrated high-margin machine model.
  * **Facebook:** Started small by seeding networks selectively (e.g., Harvard) rather than instantly aggregating massive, unfocused user pools like MySpace.
* **"Better" is Not a Moat:** 
  Operational effectiveness (being better or faster) is a benefit, not a defensive barrier. Startups relying solely on product superiority risk getting quickly replicated or crushed by deep-pocketed incumbents who can easily absorb losses or copy features (e.g., Meta launching *Muse* to counter new AI assistants).
* **The Freedom of Having Nothing to Protect:** 
  Entrants win not necessarily because they write better code, but because they have no legacy baggage, profit pools, or expensive infrastructure to cannibalize or protect.
