Lately, I have become more and more interested in how teams are using AI inside *real* production workflows. Sure, skills and AGENTS files are cool, but I want to see how this tech graduates into stuff we run on servers, reliably, all the time, creating value. I have found that a lot of the gap between experiments and production agents is pretty much old school (boring?) engineering: deterministic orchestration, observability, governance, and a few design choices borrowed from evergreen infra work, like splitting control plane and data plane. Today I tried to put all together in an article that should work as a primer for people who want to graduate their setups into scalable, production-ready software. The article covers:
* **observability** — what to watch on agentic runs, plus a practical checklist.
* **architecture** — how keep sensitive data where it lives, and separating workers from orchestration.
* **when to put a gate** — when to put a gate, and how…

#### [Raul Junco](https://substack.com/@rauljuncov/note/c-349430389?utm_source=feed-email-digest)
![Raul Junco avatar](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ue6D!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F45a92f5e-1e2e-4dfa-9ff3-45fc5ad0c57e_612x612.png)

My system-design study plan is 20% components and 80% connections. That’s why I stopped studying the fundamentals as a flat list. 
1. **Data:** Storage models, schemas, indexes, caching, and access patterns. 
2. **Distribution:** Consistency trade-offs, replication, network partitions, sharding, idempotency, locking, and versioning. 
3. **Communication:** Network basics, REST and gRPC contracts, queues and pub/sub, event-driven flows, background jobs and sagas. 
4. **Reliability:** Redundancy and failover, backups and recovery plans, rate limits, load shedding and backpressure. 
5. **Operations:** Metrics, logs, traces, alerts, safe deploys and rollbacks. 
6. **Security:** Identity, permissions, and where one ends and the other begins. 

The clusters overlap on purpose. Caching affects consistency, while sharding changes transaction guarantees. Asynchronous workflows introduce new failure states, and trust boundaries determine where authorization must be enforced.

PM is now the slowest part of an AI-native team. Here's how to run it with agents instead: I've spent months building and testing PM loops in Claude Code. These are the 12 that earned a permanent spot, and the system they add up to. 

1. **Loops gather every signal**  
   This is where most of your week goes today: reading tickets, chasing sales for deal notes. Six loops take that over:
   * **Feedback digest:** support tickets and interview notes, grouped into themes
   * **Deal intelligence:** what sales won and lost last week, and why
   * **Competitive brief:** what rivals shipped
   * **Metric anomaly flag:** pings you the day a number moves
   * **Onboarding friction:** where new users stall
   * **Customer call list:** who changed behavior, with the outreach drafted  
   Most B2B PMs hear about deals once a quarter. With the deal loop, you hear on Monday. 

2. **One agent ranks, a second checks**  
   A single agent ranking your backlog will confidently rank the wrong thing. So split the job. The maker ranks. The checker grades that ranking…
