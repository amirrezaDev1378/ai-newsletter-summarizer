# What it’s like to work at an AI-native company

*Field notes from a millennial raised in pre-AI tech*

Working at Lovable hits different. 

I’ve worked and/or advised a lot of tech companies at this point. Big ones, small ones, public ones, hypergrowth ones, slow ones, and everything in between. But I’m also very much a millennial of the previous tech era. Most of my instincts about how companies are supposed to work were formed before AI: managers manage, people have clearly defined jobs, important decisions happen in meetings, information travels through org charts, and becoming more senior generally means managing more people.

And after more than a year at Lovable, I keep running into things that violate those instincts. This is the first company I’ve worked at that I would describe as AI-native. 

Most of the AI-at-work conversation is still pretty tactical: writing emails faster, generating code, summarizing meetings, making slides, researching customers, automating support. Those are productivity improvements bolted onto an organizational model that basically stayed the same.

What’s been much stranger to experience is what happens when the assumption that AI is part of everything starts changing the organization itself: what jobs look like, how people work together, who makes decisions, how information moves, and even what it means to be good at your job.

---

## Stuff I love

### We have no titles.
Yeah, I advocated for this from the jump. We have ICs, Leads, and Heads, but titles don’t get more granular than that. 

This was almost a necessity. We were growing so fast that we didn’t want to end up in a situation where an awesome Director today is still just as awesome next year, except the company has grown 10x and their scope now looks more like what another company might call a Manager.

What I didn’t fully appreciate before is how much organizational machinery and politics are attached to titles. They’re not just labels for your job. They’re status, access, information, and, very often, permission. Lovable’s org structure is almost uncomfortably flat by comparison. People opt themselves into conversations—and opt themselves out. 

### Senior people are happy as ICs.
[I’ve talked about this a lot at this point](https://substack.com/redirect/dec2dc82-e0eb-41a1-b6c0-f927d8cec49e?j=eyJ1IjoiOTI4ejYxIn0.tSgkqY3JYQitX21MR6bNgb_LPnkIwNwTgbZDYVmbios) and it’s becoming more and more true. There are a surprising number of very senior people who are in IC roles at Lovable. Like, they had *dozens* of people under them in previous roles, but now they’re just crushing projects and tasks directly.

Some of this comes from AI capabilities expanding what any one individual can do: One highly capable person can now research, build, analyze, write, prototype, launch, and iterate in ways that used to require a much larger group. My own research study on this found that [71% of people (including managers) want to spend at least half their time on IC work](https://substack.com/redirect/e47c0a3c-42a4-41c5-8abd-41278354a739?j=eyJ1IjoiOTI4ejYxIn0.tSgkqY3JYQitX21MR6bNgb_LPnkIwNwTgbZDYVmbios).

### Everyone is very suspicious of meetings.
I spend less than 20% of my time in meetings, which would have been straight-up unimaginable in some of the companies I worked at before. Meetings are just not the default way to share information.

Some of this is because our AI tools make it much easier to share context via shared knowledge bases. But the biggest reason is that agency and autonomy are *highly* encouraged. You don’t need a permission slip to move forward, or to send every decision traveling up and down the org for approval.

## Stuff that’s hard (for old-tech me)

### The pace is insane.
I often say that I’ve *survived* at Lovable for over a year. I only half joke. Everyone in tech and especially in AI gets very hyped about the shipping velocity and how quickly innovation happens. But it is exhausting.

If the big labs drop a new model, maybe the core capabilities of our product are very different overnight, or it allows competitors to do stuff that used to be impossible. I took a 1-week vacation a few months ago and when I came back I literally felt like I was re-onboarding into a totally new company.

### Roles and responsibilities are blurry.
There are barely any boundaries between jobs at Lovable. People jump into things that are not technically theirs, start projects, build things, fix things, occasionally duplicate work, and step on each other’s toes. 

While it encourages high agency, it can also be very draining. There are reasons why companies spend a lot of time defining ownership. Sometimes 'high agency' is just a flattering way of describing duplicated work or unclear accountability.

### We’re in a constant state of reorg. 
At Lovable, the only constant is change. Teams change all the time. Responsibilities move, people shift areas, new teams appear, old ones disappear. A reorg isn’t really treated as an *event* here; it’s closer to a normal operating condition based on the assumption that structure *will* and *must* change as the context shifts.

## Stuff that’s weird

### There are tons of cowboys.
Lovable has a lot of [what I would call cowboys](https://substack.com/redirect/609727c5-ebf9-4537-9371-3e5b56a4dc94?j=eyJ1IjoiOTI4ejYxIn0.tSgkqY3JYQitX21MR6bNgb_LPnkIwNwTgbZDYVmbios)—high-agency generalist types who see something that needs to happen and just yeehaw, go chase after it without waiting for a plan. While common in early startups, it's less common in scale-ups, requiring a delicate balance as we build more reliable infrastructure.

### People are automating themselves out of their jobs. And are excited about it. 
One of the stranger things about Lovable is how normal it is for someone to join and immediately start asking, ‘How much of my own job can I automate with AI?’ 

“Automate your own job” sounds terrifying if the implied second half is *“...and then we no longer need you.”* It feels completely different if the second half is *“...so you can go figure out what your job should become next.”*

### AI agents are actual coworkers… with parents.
We have agents all over the company: Marketing agents, analytics agents, codebase agents, and a general Company Brain. I interact with AI agents almost as much as I interact with humans during a normal workday.

We manage them through a role we call an **‘agent parent.’** This is the human who’s responsible for the quality of the agent’s output—teaching it, updating it, and correcting it when it gives bad answers. If you’re the person who has answered the same question 50 times, you become the parent so you don't have to answer it a 51st time.

![Agent parent illustration](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ZDyy!,w_322,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4167476c-7d36-43cf-a1d9-93439673d463_500x889.png)

### The judgement check is just ‘Is it lovable?’
One of the phrases you hear constantly is, ‘Is this lovable?’ There is no checkbox for lovability. It forces you past functional correctness into something much harder to define: Is it delightful? Is it intuitive? Would you be excited to use it? 

---

## This is NOT an all-encompassing recommendation

The weird paradox is that the more AI removes the mechanics of work, the more the company seems to depend on human judgment. When information is everywhere, execution is cheap, and everyone has enormous leverage, knowing *what* to do, *when* to interfere, *what good looks like*, and *when something is actually done* becomes disproportionately important.

So I don’t think you can take one of these things, drop it into another company, and expect the same result. Whatever this is, it seems to work as a system. I have exactly one data point here, so consider these field notes, not a scientific pattern.
